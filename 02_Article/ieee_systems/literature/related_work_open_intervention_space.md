## Draft text (Section II, Related Work; plain formal English, no first person, no em dash)

### A. Closed and open decision spaces

Decision analysis has long assumed that the set of alternatives is given before evaluation begins. Arbel and Tong [x] identified options generation as a neglected phase that precedes choice resolution and proposed an interactive aid in which hierarchical prioritization and sensitivity analysis prompt the decision maker to enlarge the option set, while leaving generation itself to the human. Colorni and Tsoukias [x], [x] later formalized the construction of the alternative set as a recursion of decision problems, distinguished known, partially known, and unknown decision spaces, and argued that unknown alternatives arise only through backtracking and through revision of the separable dimensions that describe the set. Hofstede [x] placed the same distinction at the level of system design: a planner faces an open problem whose boundaries are decided at planning time, whereas a developer builds a formal problem with no loose ends, and planning systems that hard-wire the open aspects are not adopted. Cognitive evidence points the same way. Del Missier et al. [x] show that option generation in ill-structured problems depends on ideation beyond memory retrieval, that individuals cover only part of the available option space, and that generating more options raises the quality of the best option while lowering the mean quality of the set. Together these works motivate a decision support system that generates alternatives during operation, and they also set the condition under which such generation is safe: broad generation must be paired with strict, external evaluation.

### B. Mechanisms that open the space

Three families of mechanisms have been used to open a decision space. The first searches a fixed parameter space for structurally different solutions. Fazlollahi and Vahidov [x] argued that an active DSS should generate diverse alternatives and keep their generation separate from their evaluation, and operationalized this with a genetic algorithm whose fuzzy fitness was computed through a Monte Carlo business simulator; modeling to generate alternatives follows the same idea in mathematical programming [x]. The second composes alternatives from modules. Levin [x] characterizes the shift from choosing among given alternatives to synthesizing composite alternatives, in which a solution is assembled from design alternatives for each component under compatibility constraints, and Struss [x] realizes therapy proposal as model composition over a library of processes whose causal completion is bounded by action triggers, with plan evaluation kept as a separate, prediction-based step. The third extends the action model itself at run time, as in open-world planning and action discovery [x], [x]. In all three families the space that is opened remains bounded by a basis the system already understands: a decision vector, a module library, or a set of action schemas. The open space is an open composition over a closed and verified basis, and this is the sense in which the present work uses the term.

### C. Wildfire and disaster decision support with learned and generative components

Recent wildfire and disaster systems have added learned and generative components, but the intervention space has stayed closed or unchecked. Dubey and Dubey [x] couple a Transformer forecaster with a Mamdani rule base of fifteen expert-defined rules that maps predicted risk to four fixed response levels, and they name the handcrafted rule base as a limitation under unusual conditions. Chandra et al. [x] combine an SSN ontology of 110 SWRL rules with stream processing and a retrieval-based language model, so that suggested actions are drawn from a predefined rule base and checked only for consistency with the ontology. Cheng et al. [x] use language models to assign AHP weights over a fixed hierarchy of sixteen indicators and verify them only by correlation with a single expert table. Gerolimos et al. [x] argue for open world machine learning in disaster management, but the openness concerns the recognition of previously unseen event classes, while the response stage remains an unspecified mapping to familiar actions. At the other extreme, Sandeep et al. [x] and Tan [x] let multi-agent language models produce free-text response plans; hallucination is contained by retrieval grounding, output schemas, self-correction, or a post-hoc statistical grading of descriptive claims, and plan quality is assessed qualitatively or after generation. Cui et al. [x] survey this landscape and conclude that hallucination risk calls for external verification through physics-based constraints and deterministic simulation, yet their own decision-support case confines the agent to a fixed library of engineering tools. Simulator-centred systems are the closest neighbours. WildfireKR [x] uses an LLM agent with schema-constrained tool calls to automate calibrated cellular-automaton spread simulation, reducing scenario setup from hours to minutes, but it produces suppression-free envelopes and leaves intervention design to the analyst; the Cariri Wildfire Monitor [x] stops at a schema-constrained alert with a probability and an explanation.

### D. Position of this work

The gap that remains is a system in which (i) the intervention space is open at run time, (ii) every new intervention is built from effects a physical model can execute, and (iii) admission is decided by the physical model rather than by the proposer, by a schema, or by the decision maker alone. DisasterAware fills this gap. Its decision core starts from five seed rules and grows only on demand: stage one tunes the consequents and membership boundaries of existing rules, stage two writes a rule on an uncovered situation, and stage three asks a generative proposer for a rule the current vocabulary cannot express, alone or together with one new object, a macro intervention that bundles existing families, an intermediate concept over existing features, or a sited intervention composed from seven physical effects (wet, clear, ignite, coat, evacuate, prime, draft) placed on seven sectors of the fire. The proposer never defines a new physical effect. The simulation core, which already implements and prices each effect, is the verifier: a candidate is admitted only after it clears deterministic gates on form, vocabulary, relevance, and pool economy, and then lowers the forecast physical cost on two independently reseeded shadow forecasts. In the terms of Colorni and Tsoukias, the admission test is a rating against an external norm rather than a client's satisfaction; in the terms of Hofstede, the action set is open while the objective stays formal and the commander retains authority over the fielded plan. The decision layer therefore learns which compositions of its physical primitives help, and the simulator, not the language model, decides what enters doctrine.

## Notes for the authors

1. The strongest claim that survives review is "open composition over a closed, verified basis", not "unbounded openness". Colorni and Tsoukias would classify effect-by-sector composition as combinatorial expansion within known dimensions; the stronger case for genuinely new dimensions is the generated intermediate concept (interface_exposure) which adds an antecedent axis.
2. State explicitly that the objective J stays fixed and formal; only the intervention space is open. This pre-empts the Hofstede-style objection and keeps the paper away from "the system changes its own goals".
3. Hofstede's greenhouse case is a warning: hard gates the commander cannot override were rejected by users. One sentence on commander override relative to the gates is needed in Section III or IV.
4. WildfireKR is the natural direct comparison: same simulator-centred design, same calibration logic on national historical fires, but no intervention space. Worth a short paragraph rather than a single sentence.
5. Bibliographic cautions: Struss is QR2019, not 2020. Levin is an arXiv preprint (1408.5492). The Cariri Wildfire Monitor PDF carries no venue, year, or DOI; confirm before citing. Dubey & Dubey has internal numeric inconsistencies (92.4 vs 94 percent); cite only the table values.

Papers from the curated list not yet in the folder that would complete the mechanism story with one citation each: Brill et al. 1990 (MGA, IEEE TSMC), Chrpa & Vallati 2016 (plan synthesis for incident management), Yeh & Chang 2012 (online synthesis of emergency procedures from actuator primitives), Ding et al. 2022 COWP and Gizzi et al. 2021 (run-time action discovery), Tang et al. 2023 (factored action spaces), Wang et al. 2024 CodeAct (composable action space).
