## ABSTRACT (replaces paragraph #7)

Abstract—Wildfire response is a system-of-systems problem in which data providers, a hazard model, distributed decision agents, and a commander must act together under time pressure. Operational decision support systems choose from closed catalogues of predefined actions, cannot revise their decision logic during an incident, and rarely expose the reasoning behind a recommendation. This article presents DisasterAware, a distributed decision support architecture built on two separated cores: a simulation core that owns the hazard state and a decision core that reads it only through confidence-rated observations. Local agents turn ten observation features into five decision concepts, a sparse Takagi-Sugeno rule base maps the concepts to six intervention families, and a coordination layer ranks regions and shares capacity while the commander supervises through bounded, reversible inputs. The central contribution is an open decision space. When the standing decision falls short, a staged loop tunes the rules, writes rules for uncovered situations, and lets a pretrained language model propose new rules, intermediate concepts, and interventions composed from verified physical effects. Every proposal must clear deterministic gates and a paired forecast test on two reseeded futures before it acts, so the doctrine grows only as the incident demands, life-safety orders are never attenuated, and every order stays traceable to its concepts, rules, and confidences. The simulation core reproduces four historical fires with 86 to 94 percent agreement. On that ground, over {{NSEEDS}} paired worlds per scenario family, the language model issued {{tried3.DA}} proposals and the gates admitted {{acc3.DA}}. DisasterAware extinguishes {{succ.Avg.DA}} percent of the incidents within the episode where the static doctrine extinguishes {{succ.Avg.F5}} percent and the self-tuning doctrine {{succ.Avg.F5Ev}} percent, burns {{red.DA.F5Ev}} percent less area than the self-tuning doctrine and {{red.DA.T0}} percent less than no action, leaves {{popred.DA.F5}} percent fewer people affected than the static doctrine, and carries sixteen times more agents at under 1.3 times the cost per decision.

Index Terms—System-of-systems architecture, distributed decision support, open decision space, generative rule proposal, verification and fail-safe design, human-supervised emergency response, wildfire management.

## I. INTRODUCTION (paragraphs #31-#39 replaced by the following)

DisasterAware answers these gaps with an architecture whose central contribution is the open decision space: the set of orders the system may issue is not fixed in advance but grows during the incident, under verification by the hazard model. This article asks what that architecture is worth to the commander, and it answers with paired measurements: the same fires are replayed without decision support, with a static doctrine, with a doctrine that tunes itself, and with DisasterAware, so that the benefit of each capability is read from the difference it makes on identical worlds. Three contributions match the gaps of Table I one to one.

Open decision space through staged, verified adaptation. To address G1, the fixed menu of alternatives becomes an open field of orders: ten observation features aggregate into five decision concepts, so rules are written in operational language. The field is reshaped online on three levels: an evolving Takagi-Sugeno base adapts memberships and consequents [26, 27], term resolution grows where situations are ambiguous, and a generative stage driven by a pretrained language model may add rules, intermediate concepts, bundles of existing interventions, and new interventions composed from verified physical effects; nothing enters the base without a gate chain ending in a reseeded forecast comparison [28]. Local agents do all reasoning, while a non-inferential global layer ranks priorities, assigns resources, and adjusts acceptance gates.

Sensitivity analysis with traceability and confidence-gated uncertainty. To address G2, explanation and uncertainty handling are structural. Epistemic uncertainty is propagated without prior distributions by blending each concept activation with a persistence-based estimate in proportion to observation confidence. Every decision traces to its concepts, rules, and confidences, every admitted edit to the deficit that triggered it and the gate that judged it, and a sensitivity analysis quantifies how the loop parameters affect the outputs, in contrast to post-hoc attribution [4].

End-to-end measurement over separated cores. To address G3, the two cores are strictly separated, validated independently, and exercised end to end with an operator dashboard, every intervention screened before fielding. The decision layer is then measured on five scenario families against three references that share the world, the ignitions, and the random stream: no decision support, the static seed doctrine, and the self-tuning doctrine. Differences arise from the issued orders alone [13, 14].

This work is a systems contribution rather than a new inference algorithm. It defines the open decision space as an architectural object, namely the set of orders the system may issue at a given time together with the rules under which that set may be extended, states the invariants this object preserves, proves them for the acceptance and fail-safe gates of Section IV-H, and measures what the resulting architecture buys on an independently validated hazard model. The concept-driven inference and the generative proposer are components inside the architecture; either can be replaced without affecting the invariants.

## II. RELATED WORK AND BACKGROUND (heading replaces "II. Background"; II.A kept; II.B replaced by B, C, D below; old II.C, II.D, II.E become E, F, G with their text unchanged)

### B. Closed and Open Decision Spaces

Decision analysis has long assumed that the set of alternatives is given before evaluation begins. Arbel and Tong [35] identified options generation as a neglected phase that precedes choice, Colorni and Tsoukias [36, 37] formalized the construction of the alternative set as a recursion of decision problems and distinguished known, partially known, and unknown decision spaces, and Hofstede [38] observed that planning systems which hard-wire the open aspects of a problem are not adopted. Cognitive evidence points the same way: option generation in ill-structured problems depends on ideation beyond memory retrieval, individuals cover only part of the available option space, and generating more options raises the quality of the best option while lowering the mean quality of the set [39]. Fire produces conditions no predefined alternative covers, and commanders build courses of action from recognized patterns [31]; a closed space can only rank what it was given [15]. These works motivate a decision support system that generates alternatives during operation, and they set the condition under which generation is safe: broad generation must be paired with strict, external evaluation.

### C. Mechanisms That Open a Decision Space

Three families of mechanisms open a decision space. The first searches a fixed parameter space for structurally different solutions, as in the active DSS of Fazlollahi and Vahidov [40], whose genetic generator was kept separate from a Monte Carlo evaluator, and in modeling to generate alternatives [41]. The second composes alternatives from modules, in Levin's synthesis of composite alternatives under compatibility constraints [42] and in Struss's model composition over a library of processes with a separate, prediction-based evaluation [43]. The third extends the action model itself at run time, as in open-world planning and action discovery [44, 45]. In all three the space that is opened stays bounded by a basis the system already understands, so the open space is an open composition over a closed and verified basis, which is the sense used here. Two design consequences follow: generation must be cheap to reject, because most of the composition space leaves the outcome unchanged, and the evaluator must be independent of the generator. Evolving Takagi-Sugeno models create rules online within the existing vocabulary [27], value-based selectors learn when to change structure [32], and generative models draft rules outside the current structure [33]; DisasterAware combines the three behind a satisficing acceptance gate [28].

### D. Wildfire and Disaster Decision Support With Learned and Generative Components

Recent wildfire and disaster systems have added learned and generative components, but the intervention space has stayed closed or unchecked. Dubey and Dubey [12] couple a Transformer forecaster with fifteen expert rules that map predicted risk to four fixed response levels and name the handcrafted rule base as a limitation under unusual conditions; Chandra et al. [46] draw suggested actions from a predefined ontology of 110 rules and check them only for consistency; Cheng et al. [47] let language models weight a fixed hierarchy of indicators and verify the weights only against one expert table; Gerolimos et al. [48] call for open-world learning in disaster management, but the openness concerns the recognition of unseen event classes while the response stays a mapping to familiar actions. At the other extreme, multi-agent language models produce free-text response plans whose hallucinations are contained by retrieval grounding, output schemas, or post-hoc grading, and whose quality is assessed qualitatively or after generation [49, 50]; Cui et al. [51] conclude that external verification through physics-based simulation is required, yet confine their own agent to a fixed tool library. WildfireKR [52] is the closest neighbour, a language-model agent that automates calibrated spread simulation, but it produces suppression-free envelopes and leaves intervention design to the analyst. The gap that remains is a system in which the intervention space is open at run time, every new intervention is built from effects a physical model can execute, and admission is decided by the physical model rather than by the proposer, by a schema, or by the decision maker alone. DisasterAware fills this gap, and Section V measures what it is worth against the doctrine alone.

## VI. DISCUSSION: SYSTEMS LESSONS AND LIMITATIONS (both paragraphs replaced)

Three lessons transfer beyond wildfire. First, separating an authoritative hazard model from the decision layer is what makes both testable: the model was validated against satellite records with no decision logic in the loop, and the decision layer was then measured against fixed references on the same worlds, so the benefit of each capability is a paired difference rather than an absolute score. Second, a vocabulary of orders that grows at runtime is safe only when admission is decided by gates that do not trust the proposer; more than half of the proposals are rejected here, most of them at the package margin after they had passed the forecast tests, and that selectivity is why the admitted objects can be fielded and why the outcome improves rather than merely changes. Third, keeping inference in the regional agents and out of the coordinator held the cost per decision almost flat as the number of agents grew, which is the property an incident of national scale requires.

Three limitations bound the claims. The evaluation is entirely simulated: the hazard model is validated on real records, but no order was executed in a real incident and no fire officer took part in the loop. The comparison base is internal, since no deployed tool generates interventions over an open space; the paired ladder isolates the contribution of each capability, and the greedy allocation of Section V-F bounds what undivided aggression buys in this cost model. Finally, the proposals of a language model are not bitwise reproducible, although the campaign is logged with pinned configuration and seeds, and no admission depends on the proposer. The proposer does set the size of the margin: in a separate campaign of 50 worlds per family, a deterministic template writer behind the same gates reached 76 ha against 83 ha for F5+Ev, so the mechanism alone buys part of the margin reported here and the quality of the proposals the rest.

## VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK (paragraph replaced)

This article presented DisasterAware, a distributed decision support architecture for wildfire response built on two separated cores. Ten confidence-gated features feed five decision concepts, a sparse Takagi-Sugeno rule base sets intervention intensities, satisficing acceptance and a graduated fail-safe screen every order, and a non-inferential coordinator ranks the local agents. When the standing decision falls short, a three-stage ladder adapts the parameters, the resolution, and finally the vocabulary of the base, the last through a pretrained language model whose every proposal passes the same gates, which preserve the four invariants of Section IV-H. On an independently validated core, whose four blind hindcasts agree with the satellite record at 86 to 94 percent, the benefit was measured on paired worlds: DisasterAware extinguishes {{succ.Avg.DA}} percent of the incidents within the episode against {{succ.Avg.F5}} percent for the static doctrine and {{succ.Avg.F5Ev}} percent for the self-tuning doctrine, leaves {{popred.DA.F5}} percent fewer people affected than the static doctrine, lowers the burned area by {{red.DA.T0}} percent against no action and by {{red.DA.F5Ev}} percent against the self-tuning doctrine, admits {{acc3.DA}} of {{tried3.DA}} proposals of the language model through gates that trust none of them, and does so at 274 ms per decision and at most 1.29 times the cost for sixteen times the agents, while every fielded order traces to its concepts, rules, and confidences. Four studies follow. A tabletop exercise will place fire officers in the loop with and without the framework. A transfer study will keep the decision layer and replace the simulation core with another spreading hazard, the rewriting cost separating what is architecture from what is wildfire. Inside the simulator, an ensemble study will test decision stability across plausible forecasts, and a scenario study will build families in which the seed doctrine is structurally insufficient, so that the tactics the open space composes can be measured against the tactics it was not given.
